## Q1 — Can the Board delegate §4.7 membership-discipline to a committee by resolution?

**Answer: not by ordinary resolution — because the Board can amend the
bylaws itself.**

The chain:

- §4.4 (involuntary emeritus) and §4.7 (termination) vest these powers in
  "the members of the corporation" by two-thirds vote.
- §5.1 gives the Board all corporate powers *except* those "specifically
  reserved to the members" — which these are.
- §5.9 (committees) only lets a committee exercise "the power and
  authority of the Board of Directors" — a members' power can't pass
  through it.
- **However — Article X:** "These Bylaws may be altered, amended or
  repealed by the Board of Directors or by the members" — Board amendment
  requires only a good-faith attempt at 15 days' notice to members. No
  membership vote, no supermajority stated. Both existing appended
  amendments (2002, 2020) were Board-only resolutions.
- This Board amendment power is valid under DGCL §109(a) because the ASF
  Certificate of Incorporation, Art. 6, expressly confers it. Members
  retain concurrent power to amend (and could re-amend anything back —
  §109(a): conferral "shall not divest the ... members of the power").

**Consequence for the framework:** a delegated "committee with teeth" is
legally reachable by a single Board-adopted bylaws amendment. But the
political analysis reverses the legal one: the Board using its unilateral
amendment power to move membership-discipline authority away from the
members — the members' own §4.4/§4.7 protection — would be explosive, and
members could simply amend it back. Document-and-refer (Q2) remains the
sound design; the difference is the submission can now say "the Board
*could* delegate by amendment but should not," rather than "delegation is
a years-long proposition."

Sources: [Bylaws](https://www.apache.org/foundation/bylaws.html) §§4.4,
4.7, 5.1, 5.9, Art. X;
[Certificate of Incorporation](https://www.apache.org/foundation/records/certificate.html)
Art. 6;
[DGCL §109(a), §114, §141](https://delcode.delaware.gov/title8/c001/).

## Q2 — Does a document-and-refer committee raise any legal issue?

**Answer: no — investigate-and-refer is the legally safe side of a
well-marked line.**

DGCL §141(c) formality (board resolution, governing-body members) is
required only for committees exercising *board* powers — deciding,
binding, approving. A body that only gathers facts and refers a
recommendation to the organ that actually votes exercises no board power;
Delaware routinely recognizes purely advisory committees.

## Q3 — Path B: can the Board direct the President to adopt and publish, superseding the current CoC?

**Answer: yes — and the current CoC was never Board-adopted in the first
place.**

- The current CoC was published December 2014 by **officer action** — the
  EVP announced it; ComDev handled feedback; the Nov/Dec 2014 board
  minutes contain **no resolution and no discussion of it**. There is no
  Board-adopted CoC to outrank. The Anti-Harassment Policy was likewise
  adopted by the Conference Committee (2011), not the Board.
- Bylaws §6.2 makes the President CEO with "general and active management
  ... subject to the direction of the Board"; §13.5 allows delegated
  authority to be "general or confined to specific instances." The
  governance page states the Board "delegates authority to set and execute
  corporate policy within each officer's specific area of responsibility"
  — standing officer policy authority is normal ASF practice (VP Legal
  Affairs "sets legal policy"; ConCom amended the anti-harassment policy
  autonomously).
- The Board has already used the light mechanism on this exact topic: the
  2025-09-24 vote "asking [the EVP] to drive the process forward" was a
  minuted discussion-item vote, not a formal resolution.

## Q4 — Procedural floor before a §4.7 / §4.4 vote

**Answer: Delaware statute is silent; the common law is not. "Conservative
assumption" is the correct floor, and writing it down makes it binding.**

- No DGCL member-expulsion due-process statute (§114 just translates
  stock-corporation provisions). Delaware never adopted the Model
  Nonprofit Corporation Act's "fair and reasonable" expulsion standard
  that most states have (15-day notice + opportunity to be heard safe
  harbor).
- What courts apply instead: (1) **contract** — certificate and bylaws are
  a contract with members; expulsion must follow the org's own
  instruments; (2) the **common law of voluntary associations** — courts
  set aside expulsions that violate the org's own rules, deny "natural
  justice" (notice of charges + opportunity to be heard), or are in bad
  faith. ASF membership carries governance rights but not livelihood — so
  strict bylaws compliance + basic notice-and-hearing, not lawyers and
  cross-examination apply.

## Q5 — The "2-year inactivity → involuntary emeritus" precedent

**Answer: the specific claim is refuted — no 2-year rule, nothing
automatic — but an inactivity practice does exist, confirmed by direct VP
Legal input (2026-07-31): long-inactive members are given notice and can
respond that they still want to remain ASF members.**

What the bylaws provide (full membership article retrieved):

- **§4.3 — voluntary:** a member converts to emeritus by their own 10-day
  written, signed notice. No vote.
- **§4.4 — involuntary:** two-thirds vote of the members. No trigger
  conditions stated — inactivity or otherwise.
- No inactivity period or time-based conversion of any kind appears in the
  bylaws; the glossary describes the inactivity link loosely ("a long
  time"). **There is no fixed 2-year horizon**

**Confirmed practice:** members inactive for a long period — **five
years**, — are noticed and given the chance to say they still want to
remain ASF members before any conversion. Public corroboration of the
mechanism exists: "in a few cases, the membership has involuntarily moved
non-participating members to emeritus, primarily to ensure that future
membership meetings can reach quorum requirements"

— note both that *the membership* acts (not an officer, not automatically)
and that the recorded motivation is **quorum management**. The open
question is which bylaws door the practice runs through:

1. **Deemed-voluntary (§4.3):** non-response treated as electing emeritus.
   Administratively clean, but §4.3 requires the member's own written,
   signed notice — silence isn't that; vulnerable if a converted member
   ever objected.
2. **Batch §4.4 vote:** non-responsive names put to an omnibus
   involuntary-conversion vote at a members' meeting. Legally solid.

**Why the distinction matters for Layer 4:** under shape 2, the practice
is a genuine procedural precedent for conduct-based involuntary
conversion. Under shape 1, it is precedent only for the
*notice-and-opportunity-to-respond* step — though that is itself valuable:
the Q4 due-process floor already operating as existing ASF culture, making
codification continuity rather than innovation. One note for the framework
either way: the existing practice's recorded purpose is quorum management;
extending the same machinery to conduct cases is a purpose extension and
should be presented as such, not as "we already do this."

## Q6 — Emeritus reactivation

**Answer: not automatic, not by mere request.**

§4.5: reinstatement requires the emeritus member's written request **plus
a new membership application plus an affirmative majority vote of the
members**. Practice matches (2019 board minutes: the emeritus member
emails members@ and is added to the next new-member ballot). So
reactivation is effectively re-election at a majority threshold — lower
than the two-thirds needed to convert someone out, but a real gate.

## Q7 — 501(c)(3) obligations

**Answer: confirmed — essentially nothing. Member discipline is a
state-law matter.**

The federal tests (exempt purpose, private inurement, private benefit,
§4958, lobbying/campaign limits) say nothing about membership discipline;
no revocation ruling or case grounded on inconsistent member discipline
was found.

## Q8 — Removing non-chair Board-appointed officers

**Answer: §6.5 is the whole mechanism — pure Board discretion, no defined
process.**

"Any officer or agent and any member of a Project Management Committee
elected or appointed by the Board of Directors may be removed by the Board
whenever, in its judgment, the best interests of the corporation will be
served thereby." No cause requirement, no notice, no hearing, no
conduct-review trigger anywhere in the bylaws.

**Consequence for Layer 3:** the framework doesn't need to invent removal
authority — it exists and is total. What's missing is only a defined
*path from complaint to Board consideration*: who receives a conduct
complaint about an officer, who assembles the record, and how it reaches
the Board agenda. That's process design and it connects directly to Q9.

## Q9 — Conflicted reporting path

**Answer: reporting directly to the Board is not just a reasonable
fallback — it's the structurally correct one, and nothing blocks writing
it down.**

Follows from Q8: the Board appoints and removes every officer (§§6.4, 6.5)
and all presidential authority is "subject to the direction of the Board"
(§6.2). When the President or EVP is the subject, the only body with
authority over them is the Board — so board@ (or a designated
non-conflicted director) is the right named alternate. No bylaws change,
no legal dependency; a two-sentence CoC addition. The September 2025 wiki
draft's Recusal section handles conflicted *handlers*; this closes the
conflicted-*recipient* gap.

## Q10 — The conduct-action log

**Answer: real exposures, all manageable by design — except one genuinely
open question (GDPR).**

- **Discoverability:** a volunteer-maintained log is an ordinary
  discoverable business record — no privilege. Every entry should be
  written expecting adverse counsel to read it. Litigation hold must
  override any retention schedule (18 U.S.C. §1519 applies to nonprofits).
- **Defamation:** common-interest privilege protects need-to-know sharing;
  lost via malice or over-publication.
- **Retention:** no statutory schedule; nonprofit practice converges on a
  written schedule applied consistently (~7 years post-resolution for
  complaint files is a common analogy point; records underlying an actual
  expulsion kept longer, as they document the corporate act). A defined
  schedule with stated rationale beats indefinite ad-hoc retention under
  both litigation and privacy framings.
- **GDPR — the open one:** Art. 3(2) reach over a US foundation with
  worldwide (including EU) participants is fact-dependent and unresolved
  for US open-source foundations generally. Free services count under Art.
  3(2)(a); episodic complaint handling is a stretch for the "monitoring"
  prong but not frivolous. If it applies: legitimate-interest basis,
  storage limitation, and data-subject access requests *against the log
  itself* (a subject could demand their file) all follow.

## Q11 — Off-platform / social-media scope

**Answer: disciplining for external conduct is legally sound if the rules
clearly say so — but the draft's "official or unofficial" blanket scope
goes beyond every surveyed peer, and the vagueness is both the legal weak
point and the documented community-revolt trigger.**

**Legal side (settled):** association discipline is contractual — an org
may sanction members for conduct outside its own spaces if its adopted
rules reach it, its own procedure is followed, and it acts in good faith.
*Dawkins v Antrobus* (CA 1881) upheld expulsion for conduct entirely
outside the club under a rule against conduct "injurious to the character
and interests of the club." No First Amendment constraint (no state
action; *Boy Scouts v. Dale* runs the other way — private associations may
exclude over members' outside expression). The jurisdictional question is
*what did the member agree to*, not *where did the conduct occur* — which
makes vague scope language ("unofficial spaces," undefined) the exact
defect that generates both the rare successful legal challenges and the
governance blowups (OpalGate 2015, Drupal/Garfield 2017, Linux kernel
2018).

**Three postures, in ascending risk:**
1. External conduct as *evidence* in internal decisions — clearly fine.
2. Internal sanctions *for* external conduct — fine if the rule text is
   clear in advance.
3. Prospective restrictions on external behavior ("no interaction…
   including social media") — the org cannot enforce this directly; it
   works only as a condition whose breach triggers further *internal*
   escalation. Notably, that draft's exact phrase is verbatim Contributor
   Covenant 2.1 enforcement-ladder boilerplate, and it is self-limiting
   there ("Violating these terms may lead to a temporary or permanent
   ban") — so it can stay, framed as a condition, not a prohibition ASF
   purports to enforce off-platform.

**Peer practice (all fetched and quoted):**
- Contributor Covenant 2.1/3.0: community spaces + "officially
  representing" only. Linux kernel: same. Debian, Rust: own venues only.
- CNCF (best-drafted middle ground): external spaces covered only when
  conduct is "directed at or are about a CNCF project, the CNCF community,
  or another CNCF community participant in the context of a CNCF
  activity."
- PSF: enumerated PSF spaces in the CoC; the off-platform reach lives in
  the *enforcement procedures* as a case-by-case test: "If the incident
  occurred outside the community, but a community member's mental health
  or physical safety may be negatively impacted if no action is taken, the
  incident may be in scope."
- **No surveyed peer claims blanket jurisdiction over "unofficial"
  spaces.** The September 2025 wiki draft's Scope section is broader than
  all of them.

**Recommended fix pattern:** ASF-managed spaces + when representing the
ASF (CC 2.1 baseline), plus a CNCF-style directed-at clause for
cross-platform harassment of community members, and/or a PSF-style
harm-triggered admissibility test in the enforcement procedures rather
than the scope section. Drop "unofficial" as a scope category. This also
matches the KB framework's checkable-fact discipline: ASF can only action
its own spaces, roles, events, and membership status.
